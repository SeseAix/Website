Introduction

A Vision for a Difficult Plot

Every meaningful landscape project begins with a vision and a challenging piece of land. This journal chronicles our intensive four-day journey to transform a forgotten, heavily overgrown and steeply inclined plot into a peaceful, Japanese-inspired sanctuary.

Facing extreme summer heat, rock-hard soil and unexpected setbacks, our team worked tirelessly to bring this hidden space back to life. Guided by the wisdom of traditional Japanese garden design and fuelled by our shared determination, we set out to turn a difficult, shadowy slope into a place of rest, reflection and lasting beauty. This is the story of how our Zen garden came to be.

Concept

Guided by Karesansui and Shakkei

Our initial idea was to design a welcoming path leading from the main trail into our area, transitioning into Tobiishi. These stepping stones are not just practical — they are meant to control the pace of your walk, encouraging a mindful, meditative transition that guides you directly to the bench and its view.

The Dry Landscape — Karesansui

We envisioned a classic Karesansui, where the space would be filled with gravel and centred around an arrangement of a few large, prominent stones. In this tradition, the gravel represents flowing water, while the stones symbolise islands or mountains, designed to aid meditation from a single viewpoint. To add a touch of greenery to this serene setup, we planned to plant an elderberry bush to the side.

The Fence — Takegaki & Shakkei

To complete the space, we wanted to build a Takegaki featuring a crescent-moon-shaped cutout. This shape echoes a similar crescent moon detail on a house within our area, evoking tranquility and the passage of time, much like the architectural windows of traditional Japanese tea houses. More importantly, the cutout serves as a frame for the scenery outside the garden — the technique of Shakkei, or "borrowed scenery," which intentionally draws the surrounding landscape into the garden to make it feel like an integral part of your own peaceful space.

Journal

Four Days on the Slope

Day 01 — Clearing the Ground

We began by cutting down the tall grass with a scythe, then used hoes and rakes to remove smaller grasses, weeds and other unwanted plants from the ground, before moving behind the fence to cut back the nettles and grass as well.

Due to the lack of rain, the ground was incredibly hard, making it exhausting and time-consuming to loosen the earth and remove a few centimetres of topsoil — we needed pickaxes alongside the hoes. We then pruned the large chestnut tree with shears to clear the view of the forest, taking our first step toward Shakkei. Finally, we broke down the hardened earth to pile it up elsewhere: sieves didn't work, so we resorted to beating and stomping the clods apart by hand. We closed the day by sawing the bamboo parts in advance, ready to start building.

Cutting back the overgrowth (left) and breaking up the rock-hard soil by hand (right).
Day 02 — Building with Bamboo

We hung a Furin at the corner of the entrance — a dual-purpose gesture that warns visitors before they bump their heads and partially blocks the direct view into the garden, adding privacy and a touch of mystery.

Building the Takegaki proved a real challenge in the extreme heat and exposed, sunny location: the specific Japanese knots took a long time to learn, and a mid-build change to the fence layout meant driving far too many wooden pegs into dry, hard earth. Once the fence was standing, we wove straw and willow branches through it for a natural privacy screen, then cut a crescent-moon shape into it — a Genji-mado that frames the view and brings Shakkei into the garden.

Building the Takegaki in the summer heat (left) and the crescent-moon Genji-mado framing the view (right).
Day 03 — The Stepping-Stone Path

The third day was dedicated to our Tobiishi, designed to direct movement and mindfully control walking speed. The path navigates the narrow gap between the two houses and allows for a gradual, deliberate reveal of the scenic field view and skyline.

We achieved this through different types of stones: long, perpendicular elements slow the visitor's pace first, before the path transitions into a tiled area of multiple stones — some already showing natural Koke — that marks the official entrance to our "secret garden," working as both a walkway and part of the landscape. From there, separate flat stepping stones guide you the rest of the way to the wooden bench.

Laying the Tobiishi (left) and the mossy stones marking the entrance to the garden (right).
Day 04 — Planting and Finishing Touches

After our large stone was unfortunately stolen, we found a replacement. We also received a Sakura from the HIWI, which we placed next to our small stone basin to form a traditional Tsukubai, then added the remaining stones to complete the Tobiishi path up to the bench. To bring more life to the space, we planted a small apple tree and distributed patches of grass throughout the area.

Realising we couldn't cover the whole plot with gravel as planned, we adapted with a smaller, dedicated gravel section, raked to give it an authentic Karesansui feel. We built a minimalist bamboo door at the entrance — a single pole resting between the two houses as a symbolic Kekkai, simply lifted aside to enter — and planted a flowering bush in the back corner, where the space had felt empty, to conceal it naturally.

To soften the edges of the water feature, we foraged for Koke in the forest and arranged it around the basin for a natural sense of age. Finally, on the recommendation of our Japanese master, we practised Ishi-gumi by placing five specific stones around our small gravel "lake" — each one representing a member of our group.

The Tsukubai with the Sakura and stone basin (left) and the raked Karesansui with its five Ishi-gumi stones (right).
Reflection

A Testament to Adaptability

The transformation of this steep, overgrown and challenging plot into a serene, Japanese-inspired garden was an unforgettable journey of resilience and teamwork. Despite the intense heat, the rock-hard soil that demanded pickaxes, and setbacks like stolen materials, our group never lost sight of our vision. Having to adapt our original plans — scaling back the Karesansui gravel area, working with limited resources — only made the final space more personal and unique.

Every detail, from the bamboo elements of the Takegaki and Furin, to the newly planted trees and the Ishi-gumi of five stones representing each of us around the gravel lake, tells the story of our shared effort. Sitting together on the bench at night, looking out through our beautifully framed Shakkei over the garden we built with our own hands, all the initial exhaustion faded into a deep, well-earned sense of pride and tranquility.

Not just a beautiful Zen garden with a stunning view — a lasting testament to hard work, adaptability and friendship.
Process

From Overgrown Slope to Zen Garden

  • 01 Clearing tall grass, weeds and nettles by scythe, hoe and rake across the plot and behind the fence.
  • 02 Loosening rock-hard, rain-starved soil with pickaxes and breaking down hardened clods by hand.
  • 03 Pruning the chestnut tree to open the forest view — the first step toward Shakkei.
  • 04 Hanging a Furin at the entrance and building the Takegaki, woven with straw and willow.
  • 05 Cutting a crescent-moon Genji-mado into the fence to frame the borrowed scenery.
  • 06 Laying the Tobiishi stepping-stone path, using stone type and spacing to control pace and reveal.
  • 07 Placing the Sakura and stone basin as a Tsukubai, and completing the path to the bench.
  • 08 Raking a scaled-back Karesansui gravel section and building a bamboo Kekkai at the entrance.
  • 09 Planting an apple tree and flowering bush, and foraging Koke to soften the basin's edges.
  • 10 Practising Ishi-gumi — placing five stones around the gravel "lake," one for each member of the group.
